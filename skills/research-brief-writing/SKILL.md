# Research brief writing

A brief exists to change a decision. Build it so a reader who stops after the first screen still knows the conclusion, how sure you are, and what to do. Lead with the point and rank everything after it by importance, the inverted-pyramid pattern recommended by Nielsen Norman Group (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/).

## 1. Before writing

1. Read the reader's own writing and evidence rules if they exist. They outrank this skill.
2. Fix the coverage window with dates, times and timezone, and list what you could not read: paywalls, failed fetches, partial access.
3. Pull every claim you plan to use from the claim ledger. Each claim needs a source URL, publication date, event date if different, access date, evidence label, and its limitation. A claim with no source does not go in.
4. Decide the one decision the brief supports. If nothing meaningful changed, say so in three lines and stop.

## 2. Evidence labels and source counting

Use exactly these labels and put one next to every factual claim:

| Label | Use when |
|---|---|
| Supported by primary evidence | You read the original document, dataset or regulation text yourself |
| Independently corroborated | Two or more origins that do not share a source agree |
| Provisional | Credible but self-reported, vendor-run, modelled, small sample or single chain |
| Disputed | Credible evidence contradicts it |
| Unverified | Asserted with no checkable support, or you only saw a summary |

Add "Analysis" for your own interpretation, so readers never mistake a hypothesis for a finding.

Counting rules:
- Count origins, not articles. Ten reports repeating one press release are one chain.
- A vendor announcement proves that something was announced, not that it performs.
- A benchmark is not production; self-report is not observation; modelled is not measured.
- Stars, likes and followers measure attention, never quality or adoption.
- State the scope a figure holds for, such as "US customers of one vendor", in the same sentence.
- Check denominators, baselines, measurement windows and correlation versus causation before you upgrade a label.
- Never invent or interpolate a number to fill a table, chart or formula.

## 3. Structure

1. **Headline sentence.** The conclusion as a full sentence, not a topic label.
2. **Opening paragraph.** What changed, why it matters, how sure you are. No "Bottom line" or "Summary" label.
3. **Summary block.** Three to five takeaways, each one sentence plus its evidence label, then the one decision for the period.
4. **At a glance table.** Topic with evidence label and independent-source count; what shifted; so what. Keep it to three columns so it reads on a phone.
5. **One card per topic, same order every time:**
   - How it works: one everyday analogy, then the precise mechanism. Never stack analogies.
   - The challenge it helps solve: the real problem a leader faces, stated concretely.
   - What is new, and the limits of the evidence: figures with labels and scope, then sources with links.
   - Best counterargument: the strongest credible objection, sourced.
   - What would change my mind: the specific result that would reverse the view.
   - Implications for an engineering leader, and for a buyer or founder, with a regional or regulatory angle where relevant.
   - One action, or an explicit reason to wait.
6. **Regulation section** when relevant. Keep similarly named things apart, for example the EU Digital Operational Resilience Act versus the DORA DevOps metrics programme.
7. **Decision model.** A formula box with every term defined, the assumptions written out, a short yes or no checklist, and what would overturn the model.
8. **Trends.** Say what social and repository signals do and do not show.
9. **Article or follow-up opportunity** with thesis, audience, counterargument and the trap to avoid.
10. **Sources earning attention**: keep, add, downgrade, with one reason each. Suggest, never unsubscribe.
11. **Action items**: sender and subject only, most urgent first. No private message content.
12. **Close** on a question or decision for the reader, then one line of material limitations. No recap.

Daily briefs: roughly 600 to 900 words, up to five developments, one visual. Weekly briefs: cross-topic synthesis plus the decision model and source review.

## 4. Visuals

Draw only when the reader learns more than from a paragraph. One message per visual, at most about nine nodes, one or two accent elements, a source line with dates, and "modelled", "vendor data" or "analysis" written on the figure when true. Use only data already in the sources. Inspect the rendered image before shipping it.

## 5. Writing rules

- Plain, specific words. No puffery, no "serves as", no forced groups of three.
- No em or en dashes. Use commas, colons or full stops, and write ranges as "2 to 5".
- Sentence-case headings. Little bold. Prose for reasoning, bullets only for real parallel lists.
- Use "I" for positions and mark them as analysis. Leave one honest open question rather than a false resolution.
- Frame problems structurally; name people for context, not blame.
- Keep employer-confidential data, private mail text and personal details out of the brief and out of public search queries.

## 6. Verification before delivery

- [ ] Every number traces to a ledger row and a link, with scope stated.
- [ ] Every claim carries a label; analysis is marked as analysis.
- [ ] Source counts follow the one-origin rule.
- [ ] Each topic has a counterargument and a "what would change my mind".
- [ ] No dashes as connectors, no generic labels, headings in sentence case.
- [ ] Visuals rendered and inspected; every figure has a source line.
- [ ] Coverage window, access gaps and corrections are disclosed.
- [ ] The brief ends on a decision or question, followed by its limitations.
- [ ] Never claim a check, save or send that you did not confirm.
